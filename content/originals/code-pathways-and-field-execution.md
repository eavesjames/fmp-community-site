## The pattern

Technology adoption in the built environment follows a consistent two-phase pattern:

**Phase 1 — Code clarity.** A standards body, legislature, or regulatory agency creates a formal pathway: a code article, a permit category, a streamlined approval process. From the policy perspective, the problem is solved. The technology is legal. The path to deployment is open.

**Phase 2 — Field execution gap.** The formal pathway exists but the field conditions required to use it do not. Inspectors are unfamiliar with the article and invent review criteria independently. Contractors lack training for the installation method. Project economics haven't yet adjusted to reflect the new pathway. Adoption lags the policy target by years.

This pattern is not unique to any one technology. It is characteristic of any technology that requires parallel development of code, training, and economics to achieve deployment at scale. Understanding it in two current cases — NEC Class 4 FMP and California heat pump permitting — provides a predictive model for DC power distribution in data centers, which is approaching Phase 1 right now.

---

## Case 1: NEC Class 4 FMP

The formal pathway for Class 4 Fault Managed Power is clear. NEC Article 726, adopted in the 2023 edition, establishes Class 4 as a recognized circuit type. UL 1400-1 is the safety standard. The wiring method, installation requirements, and permit pathway are all defined.

The field-execution gap is real and observable:

**AHJ familiarity varies widely.** Jurisdictions that have adopted the 2023 NEC are required to accept Class 4 submittals, but familiarity with Article 726 among working inspectors is uneven. A submittal that moves through quickly in one jurisdiction may require multiple resubmittals in an adjacent jurisdiction simply because the plan reviewer has not seen Class 4 before. Each resubmittal is a project delay.

**Contractor training is in early stages.** IBEW and NECA have added Class 4 content to continuing education, but the base of licensed electricians with hands-on Class 4 experience is small relative to the scale of the deployment opportunity. Contractors who could install FMP systems are currently developing that experience in the field, which introduces learning-curve costs and schedule uncertainty that experienced installations do not have.

**Economics are not yet normalized.** Project costs for Class 4 FMP installations reflect early-market conditions: higher design time, less competitive subcontractor pricing, and owner uncertainty about final permit timeline. As volume increases, these costs will normalize — but they are a real adoption barrier in the current market.

*Synthesis: Class 4 FMP is in mid-Phase 2. The policy problem is solved. The field execution is catching up. The rate of catch-up depends primarily on how quickly real projects accumulate inspection precedent that AHJs can reference, and how quickly contractor training scales.*

## Case 2: California heat pump permitting

California has made significant policy investments in heat pump adoption: streamlined permit categories for like-for-like replacements, reach code programs, and utility incentive programs. The stated goal is millions of heat pump installations.

The field-execution gap is different but structurally identical:

**Electrical upgrade costs are not solved by permit streamlining.** The primary barrier to heat pump adoption in older multifamily buildings is not the permit for the heat pump itself — it is the cost of the electrical service upgrade required to power it. Industry data shows roughly 50% of heat pump retrofit projects in older buildings are canceled because of electrical upgrade costs. Permit streamlining for the heat pump does not address the electrical upgrade cost. The two policies operate on different parts of the same problem.

**High electricity rates undermine operating cost economics.** California has some of the highest residential electricity rates in the country. Heat pump adoption depends partly on operating cost savings relative to gas. When electricity rates rise faster than gas rates, the operating cost case weakens at the same time that the policy environment is strengthening. The policy does not control the economics.

**Contractor readiness lags.** Heat pump installation at scale requires HVAC contractors who can scope electrical requirements, coordinate with electricians on panel upgrades, and navigate local utility interconnection processes. That combined capability is not uniformly available in the contractor market.

*Synthesis: California's heat pump permitting effort is a genuine policy advance. It will not achieve its stated targets on the projected timeline because it addresses Phase 1 (code clarity) more effectively than Phase 2 (field execution). The electrical upgrade barrier and operating cost economics are both outside the scope of permit streamlining.*

## Case 3: DC power distribution for data centers

DC power distribution in data centers is in late Phase 1. The technology is deployed at reference scale (Circl building in Amsterdam; Current/OS consortium deployments; EMerge Alliance-certified installations). The efficiency case is documented. The demand from AI workloads is real and growing.

The code pathway does not yet exist. There is no NEC prescriptive article for DC power distribution in premises wiring at 350–400VDC. AHJ approval for DC distribution projects in data centers currently proceeds under alternative means and methods — a longer, more variable approval path than a prescriptive article would require.

The field-execution barriers are predictable from the Class 4 and heat pump cases:

**AHJ familiarity will lag code adoption.** When a DC distribution article is adopted in the NEC, the pattern from Class 4 suggests that inspector familiarity will be uneven for 3–5 years after adoption. Early movers will bear the cost of educating inspectors.

**Contractor training does not yet exist at scale.** There is no DC distribution credential equivalent to what IBEW now has for Class 4. The training infrastructure needs to be built before the code is adopted, or field execution will lag.

**Project economics are in early-market conditions.** DC distribution projects are priced as custom engineering. As more projects are completed and inspected, costs will normalize — but early adopters pay for that normalization.

## What the pattern predicts, and what to do about it

The Class 4 and heat pump cases suggest that DC distribution will follow the same adoption curve: code adoption in 2–4 years, meaningful deployment volume lagging code adoption by 3–5 years, with the execution gap filled gradually as inspection precedent accumulates and contractor training scales.

That lag is not inevitable. It is addressable before the code is adopted. The actions that reduce adoption lag for DC distribution:

1. **Build inspection precedent now through alternative means and methods approvals.** Each project that successfully completes AHJ review under alternative means generates documentation that the next project in that jurisdiction can reference. Early projects pay the review cost; later projects benefit from the precedent.

2. **Develop contractor training before code adoption, not after.** The Class 4 experience shows that waiting for code adoption to develop training results in a skills shortage that is visible in early project economics. DC distribution training content can be developed based on current deployments without waiting for a prescriptive NEC article.

3. **Address the field economics explicitly.** For heat pumps, the electrical upgrade cost is the adoption barrier that permit streamlining cannot solve. For DC distribution, the analogous barrier is the design cost premium for early-market projects. Published cost models and reference designs reduce that premium.

*Synthesis: The policy community and the standards community tend to treat code clarity as the primary problem. The field execution community knows it is the second problem. Both are right. The difference is timing: the policy work happens in a 2-4 year cycle, while the field execution work happens in a 5-10 year accumulation. Starting the field execution work before the policy work is complete is not premature — it is the only way to avoid the adoption lag that the Class 4 and heat pump cases predict.*
